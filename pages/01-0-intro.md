## What this book is for

AI news articles mix two kinds of sentences: **verified facts** and **framing**. They sit in the same paragraph, in the same tone, at the same type size. Most readers cannot separate them, and remember what they read.

This book externalises that separation into **eight tests**. A test you can put into words can be counted while you read: *how many of the eight does this sentence break?*

## Where the eight principles come from

Between 2026-10-09 and 2026-10-17 we collected AI-subject news three times a day and critiqued the coverage — 22 editions. In each edition we copied the offending sentence verbatim, tagged it with the principle it broke, and **wrote a corrected sentence**. Eight failure types kept recurring:

| Principle | Share of violations |
|---|---|
| ③ Disclose technical limits | 27% |
| ⑤ Disclose interests | 21% |
| ⑦ Cite verifiable sources | 18% |
| ④ Give context | 17% |
| ⑧ Offer alternatives | 13% |
| ⑥ Give cost and benefit together | 11% |
| ① Do not anthropomorphise | 7% |
| ② Name who is responsible | 7% |

The chapters run in that order — most frequent failure first.

## How to judge

1. **Copy the sentence verbatim.** Do not summarise; summarising blurs the judgement.
2. **Tag the principles it breaks** — often three or four at once (e.g. `①③`).
3. **Write the corrected sentence.** If you cannot write a replacement, you have not finished judging.

## Limits

The case files are dated and will age. The principles are written so they survive their examples. This book is **not legal advice** and not a verdict on any outlet: it is sentence-level judgement practice. Cases where our own call was wrong are in the last part.
